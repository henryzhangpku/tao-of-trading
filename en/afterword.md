# Afterword: The Same Old Way

Almost everyone learns to trade the same way. You read the same books, watch the same videos and take the same advice. Have a view. Do the work. Be aggressive when you are right and cut it fast when you are wrong. Trade more to learn faster. Watch more to see sooner. Want it more than the next person.

It is not only trading. It is how most of us were taught to live. Work harder, go faster, get ahead, hold on, never give up. The trader at the screen is the same person who was praised as a child for trying harder, and he brings that lesson to the order ticket because it is the only one he was given.

Laozi was given it too, two and a half thousand years ago. His world was full of ambitious men, clever advisers and rulers who wanted more. He looked at all that effort and wrote down the other way round. The soft beats the hard. The low place receives everything. The one who stands on tiptoe cannot stand. Doing nothing, nothing is left undone. Returning is the way the Way moves.

This book has done one thing eighty-one times. It took a lesson every trader already knows and set it beside a verse that sees it the other way. None of the chapters gives you a new setup. Every one of them asks you to hold an old one in a different hand, and gives you one habit to start with.

---

I owe one book a clearer account than the prologue gave it.

Mark Spitznagel's *The Dao of Capital* (2013) reads Laozi, and the Chinese strategists after him, for the investor. Its heart is the roundabout path: give up a little now, deliberately, to arrive at a stronger position later. He joins that old idea to the Austrian economists and their *Umweg*, the detour through patient capital, and he reads the Chinese idea of *shi* 势, the force a thing has because of where it stands, as a lesson in positioning. I was very influenced by it. Where its ideas meet mine, I have tried to say so in the chapter. I have not quoted its text or tried to restate its argument; it makes that argument better than I could.

The difference between the two books is one of scale. Spitznagel's unit of time is the economic cycle, years and decades. This book's unit is the trading day, sometimes the minute: the trade not taken at the open because the screen is moving and you want to move with it; the loss taken at the price you named, not at the one you renegotiate with yourself after lunch; the afternoon you leave the screen alone. Laozi works at both scales. But the temptations feel different from the inside. An investor is tempted every few years. A trader is tempted every few minutes, in small portions, each one reasonable.

---

I first met the *Tao Te Ching* in Chinese, as a schoolchild in China, the way many Chinese children meet it: a few lines at a time, before they mean anything, so that they are there later when they do. I read it now in the original. That is not a claim to see more deeply than anyone else. Laozi is hard in Chinese too. It is only a claim about what the original gives a trader that a translation can hide.

无为 is not "inaction". It is "without forcing", and the whole difference between a trader who sits on his hands and one who is merely frozen lives in that word. 知止 is two characters, "know stopping", and a Chinese trader hears the end of the day in it at once. 知足, "know enough", is not modesty. It is the most practical line in the book. 柔 is soft, but it is the softness of water, which has never lost to a stone. The Chinese edition of this book leans on those words directly, which is why it was written by hand and not translated.

---

Two chapters near the end say the thing I most want to leave you with. Chapter 70: *my words are very easy to know and very easy to practise, yet no one under heaven can know them, no one can practise them.* Chapter 78: *that the soft overcomes the hard, everyone under heaven knows; no one can practise it.*

That is the honest problem with a book like this. You will agree with most of it. You agreed with much of it before you started. Every trader already knows that patience pays, that forcing hurts, that the urge to trade is not a reason to trade. Knowing has never been the difficulty. The difficulty is the next open, when the screen moves and every lesson you were ever taught about life tells you to move with it.

So do not try to remember the book. Remember one line at a time, the way it was first learned in China: before it means anything, so that it is there later, on the morning when it does.

Slow is fast across a decade. It is also fast across a morning, and the morning is the one you have to get through today.
