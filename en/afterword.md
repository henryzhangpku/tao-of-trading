# Afterword: Trading, Not Investing

There is a book I should have written about at more length in the prologue, and this is the place to do it properly.

Mark Spitznagel's *The Dao of Capital* came out in 2013. It is a book about investing, built on ancient Chinese thought: Laozi, and the strategists who came after him. Its central idea is the roundabout path. You give up a little now, deliberately, in order to arrive at a better position later. The direct grab for the immediate gain is the weaker move; the patient, indirect route is the stronger one. Spitznagel joins that old idea to the Austrian economists, who called the detour through patient capital the *Umweg*, and to the thought that the investor's real enemy is the hurry to be paid today. He reads the Chinese idea of *shi* 势, the force a thing has because of where it stands, as a lesson in positioning: arrange where you are before you need to act.

I was very influenced by it. I want to say that plainly, because a book that sits this close to another book owes it a clear account of the debt. If you have read *The Dao of Capital*, you will have felt its shadow in several chapters here: in the patience, in the suspicion of the direct route, in the idea that giving something up can be the stronger move. Where the ideas meet, I have tried to say so in the chapter itself. I have not quoted its text, and I have not tried to restate its argument. It makes that argument better than I could, and it is there to be read.

What I have tried to do is take the same old book somewhere else.

---

The difference is one of horizon, and it is larger than it sounds.

Spitznagel reads Laozi for capital, over years and decades. His unit of time is the cycle: the long build-up, the distortion, the correction, and the investor who was positioned for it because they gave up something for a long time beforehand. His roundabout path is measured in seasons of an economy.

This book reads Laozi for the trading day. My unit of time is the session, sometimes the hour. The roundabout path, for a trader, is not a decade of patience. It is the limit order resting where the price must come, instead of the market order that chases it. It is the trade not taken at 9:31 because the screen is moving and you want to move with it. It is the loss cut at the price you wrote down last night, not the price you renegotiate with yourself this afternoon. It is the forward test you leave alone until tomorrow, and the day after that, until the season you promised it is over.

Laozi works at both scales. That is part of what makes him strange and useful. 企者不立，跨者不行, who stands on tiptoe does not stand firm, who strides does not walk: that is true of a pension fund across a decade, and it is true of a trader across a morning. But the two scales feel different from the inside. An investor's temptation is to sell the patient position too early, after a few bad years. A trader's temptation comes every few minutes, in small portions, each one reasonable. The discipline has to be finer-grained. It has to live in the order ticket, the size field, the stop, the rule that switches the screen off. That is where this book has tried to put it.

---

There is a second difference, and it has to do with how the book was read.

Spitznagel came to Laozi, as most Western readers do, through translation. A translation is already a reading. Every English version of the *Tao Te Ching* chooses, line by line, one sense among several, and the choices add up to a different book from translator to translator.

I first met the text in Chinese, as a child, in China, the way many Chinese children meet it: a few lines at a time, before they mean anything, so that they are there later when they do. I read it now in the original. That is not a claim to see more deeply than anyone else. Laozi is hard in Chinese too, and the scholars who have spent their lives on him know things I never will. It is a claim about what this book can offer that a translation-based book cannot: the play of the original words. 知止 is two characters, "know stopping," and a Chinese trader hears the daily loss limit in it at once. 无为 is not "inaction" but "without forcing," and the difference is the whole of chapter 37. 朴 is wood before the carver; every filter added to a backtest is a cut. The Chinese edition of this book can lean on those words directly, which is why it was written by hand and not translated.

And there is the other half. I build trading systems. I spend my working life on rules that are written down, frozen, fingerprinted and tested forward on prices nobody has seen; on ledgers that count every trial, so that the luckiest of forty tries is not mistaken for the only one; on an AI agent that trades in public on written rules, where the model proposes and the rules decide, and nobody, including me, reaches in mid-test. Laozi did not know about any of this. But when I read 为者败之，执者失之, whoever forces it ruins it, whoever grips it loses it, I do not read it as a metaphor. I read it as a description of what happens when a person edits a frozen rule in the eleventh week of a twelve-week test.

So the book has tried to offer both halves first-hand: the old text, read in its own language, and the modern desk, described by someone who works at one. Neither half alone is new. Put together, I think they are.

---

There is a third difference, and it is the one that matters most now.

When *The Dao of Capital* was written, the question of who decides was simple. A person decided. The investor's patience was the investor's own.

That is no longer quite true. More and more trading is proposed by models. A language model can suggest a hundred variations on an idea overnight, and one of them will look wonderful by luck alone. A screen can surface a setup every few seconds. The pressure on a trader today is not only their own impatience; it is a machine that never gets tired of offering something to do.

Laozi turns out to be very good on this, because he was writing about rulers and the people under them, and about how the best ruler is the one who is hardly noticed. The rule I run my desk on is a modern reading of chapter 37: 道常无为而无不为, the Tao does nothing, and nothing is left undone. The model proposes. The rules decide. Every suggestion becomes a counted test, and none becomes a trade on its own. The model is allowed to be brilliant. It is not allowed to be in a hurry, because the rules are not.

That is the book's white space, as far as I can tell. There are Dao-and-markets books for the investor; Spitznagel's is the best I know. There are trading-psychology books by the shelf. I have not found a book that reads Laozi chapter by chapter, in the original, for the active trader and for the age of rules run by machines. So I wrote one.

---

None of this is a claim that one horizon is better than the other. A trader and an investor are doing different jobs, and some readers do both. If what you need is the long view of capital, read *The Dao of Capital*. If what you need is something to hold in your head at 9:29 in the morning, with an order ticket open and a screen asking you to act, I hope this book has been some use.

The old man would say the two roads are one road. He would also say not to argue about it. Slow is fast across a decade. It is also fast across a morning, and that is the one most of us have to get through today.
