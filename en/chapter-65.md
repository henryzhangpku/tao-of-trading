# 65. The Ancients Did Not Enlighten

*Part II: The Virtue · 《古之善为道者》*

## The Verse

古之善为道者，非以明民，将以愚之。民之难治，以其智多。

故以智治国，国之贼；不以智治国，国之福。

知此两者亦稽式。常知稽式，是谓玄德。玄德深矣，远矣，与物反矣，然后乃至大顺。

The ancients who were good at the way did not use it to make the people clever. They used it to keep them plain. People are hard to govern because they have too much cleverness.

So to govern a state by cleverness is to rob it. Not to govern it by cleverness is its good fortune.

To know these two is also to know the pattern. Always to know the pattern is called dark virtue. Dark virtue is deep, and far, and turns back against things, and only then reaches the great accord.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. Keep the rule plain. The ancients did not make the people clever, and a trader should not make his rules clever either.

2. A strategy too clever to explain is too clever to trust. If you cannot say in one sentence why it should work, you do not know what you are holding.

3. An account is hard to govern when it has too much cleverness in it: a filter for every loss, an exception for every regime, a special case for every name that ever hurt.

4. Each clever fix solves one past problem and adds one future surprise. That is what it means to rob the state.

5. Not governing by cleverness is good fortune. A plain rule fails in plain ways, and plain failures can be read.

6. Here is the pattern to keep: say the reason before you show the chart. If the reason needs the chart to sound true, it is the chart talking.

7. I have dropped ideas that passed their tests because I could not explain them to myself in one sentence. I do not miss them.

8. Dark virtue turns back against things. Research grows by adding. Plainness grows by taking away, and that runs against every instinct of a person who likes to build.

9. Only then the great accord. A plain rule does not fit the market. It goes along with it.

## The Story

The idea came from the desk's language model, on an ordinary night, in a list with several others. Most of the list was the usual: a threshold moved, a holding period shortened, a filter borrowed from one family of rules and tried on another. One item was stranger. It combined three things I would never have put together — how a name's trading volume had changed over a few days, where its price had closed inside the day's range, and which day of the week it was — and it said that when all three lined up in a particular way, the next few days tended to lean one way.

I almost skipped it. I ran it because the desk runs everything that is proposed; that is what the ledger is for. Every proposal becomes a counted trial, and the count is what keeps me honest about luck.

It passed the gate.

---

I should say what that means, because it is not nothing. It survived after costs and after twice the costs. It held in the first half of the history and, separately, in the second. Taking out its best month did not kill it. And the trial count for its family was low, because it was the first of its kind, so the bar it had to clear was close to the plain one. On paper it was the best-behaved new idea I had seen in a long while.

The next step would have been a forward test: freeze it, fingerprint it, let it run for a season against the random book. That costs nothing but time. I could have started it that morning.

Before anything goes forward, though, I write one sentence at the top of a page. Not what the rule does: why it should work. Who is making a mistake, or who is being paid for a service, and why that would keep happening after I start standing in the same place. The sentence is not a test. The gate is the test. The sentence is how I find out whether I know what I am about to hold.

I sat down with the page a little after five, with the coffee, and wrote nothing for a long time.

---

Then I wrote a sentence, read it back, and saw that it was a description. *It buys when volume has risen, the close is low in the range, and it is a Tuesday.* That is what. It is not why.

So I wrote a why. Something about funds that rebalance on a weekly schedule and leave a mark in the volume. It sounded good. Then I noticed that I could write a second why, about traders who sell into the close ahead of the weekend's news, that sounded just as good and pointed at different people entirely. Then a third. By the time the coffee was cold I had three explanations, each one plausible, none of which I could check, and each of which would have predicted a slightly different rule.

Three reasons for one rule is not three times the understanding. It is none. If I could explain it any way I liked, I could not explain it.

*People are hard to govern because they have too much cleverness.* The cleverness here was not in the rule. It was in me, at the page, able to supply a story for anything that had passed a test. That is the dangerous kind, because it feels like understanding and does the work of decoration.

---

I thought about what it would be like to hold it.

A plain rule fails in plain ways. When a rule that follows moves loses, I know why: the market had no moves to follow. When a reversion rule loses, I know why: the move kept going. I can read those losses, and reading them tells me whether the rule is having an ordinary bad stretch or has stopped working. That reading is most of what keeps my hands off a rule during a test.

This one would have failed in ways I could not read. A bad week would have been a bad week with no explanation, and with no explanation I would have had nothing to hold on to but the chart from the gate. I know what I do when all I have is a chart. I start adding things. *To govern a state by cleverness is to rob it.* A rule I could not explain would have become a rule I carved, one unexplained loss at a time.

So I did not send it forward. I wrote that on the page, under the three sentences, in one line: *no reason in one sentence; not promoted.*

---

It is still in the ledger. Nothing that is tried is ever taken out; the count of attempts only grows. Anyone reading the ledger can see that it was tried, that it passed the gate and that it went no further, with the reason beside it. I keep that line where I can find it, because it is the kind of decision that leaves no other trace.

I do not know whether it would have worked forward. I will not find out, and I have made my peace with that, which is a sentence I did not expect to write about an idea that passed every test I have. The rules I can explain have taught me something every time they went wrong. This one could only have taught me to want it.

The ancients, the chapter says, did not use the way to make the people clever. They kept them plain. I used to hear contempt for the people in that. Now I read it as a description of what the plain can do and the clever cannot: be understood by the one who has to live with it, on the morning it goes wrong.

*Dark virtue is deep, and far, and turns back against things.* Everything in research pushes toward adding. The hardest step I take is the one backward, away from a result I cannot explain.
