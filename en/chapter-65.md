# 65. The Ancients Did Not Enlighten

*Part II: The Virtue · 《古之善为道者》*

## The Verse

古之善为道者，非以明民，将以愚之。民之难治，以其智多。

故以智治国，国之贼；不以智治国，国之福。

知此两者亦稽式。常知稽式，是谓玄德。玄德深矣，远矣，与物反矣，然后乃至大顺。

The ancients who were good at the way did not use it to make the people clever. They used it to keep them plain. People are hard to govern because they have too much cleverness.

So to govern a state by cleverness is to rob it. Not to govern it by cleverness is its good fortune.

To know these two is also to know the pattern. Always to know the pattern is called dark virtue. Dark virtue is deep, and far, and turns back against things, and only then reaches the great accord.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

Markets reward intelligence, the way school and work always have: the smartest person in the room gets ahead. The better your analysis, the more edges you find. A good trader knows the exceptions: when a rule applies and when it does not, when a stop should be honoured and when the context says to give it room. Rigid rules are for beginners. As you get better, you learn to read the situation and adapt. The best traders can think their way through anything, always have an angle, and can explain why this time is different when it really is.

## The Other Way

1. *Not to make the people clever, but to keep them plain.* Be plain with yourself. The person you most need to govern is you.

2. *People are hard to govern because they have too much cleverness.* You are hard to govern for the same reason. You can argue with any rule you wrote.

3. *To govern a state by cleverness is to rob it.* Cleverness turned on your own rules is a thief inside the house.

4. The clever mind's favourite sentence is *this time is different.* It is sometimes true, and it is almost always said while you are losing.

5. A stop you can reason your way past is not a stop. It is an opinion with a number attached.

6. The more intelligent the trader, the better the excuses. Intelligence does not protect you from rationalising; it supplies it.

7. *Not to govern it by cleverness is its good fortune.* A dull rule you do not argue with protects you on the days your mind is working against you.

8. Keep the rules plain enough that a tired, frightened version of you can follow them without thinking.

9. *Dark virtue turns back against things.* Turning away from your own cleverness goes against everything you were taught to admire.

10. *Only then reaches the great accord.* When you stop arguing with yourself, your trading and your plan finally agree.

## The Story

You have a rule: when the price reaches the point where your idea is wrong, you get out. It is a good rule. You wrote it yourself.

Today the price reaches that point, and you notice things. The volume on the drop is light, which suggests the selling is not serious. There is a more important level a little further down, and any experienced trader knows price often tests a level before it turns. The rest of the sector is holding up. You set the stop yesterday, before this morning's news, and the news has changed the picture. Every one of these observations is reasonable. Several are even true.

So you move the stop to the important level. It is not breaking the rule, exactly. It is applying it intelligently.

---

The price reaches the important level and goes through. Now there is another rule, about the most you will lose in a day, and you are close to it. You think about that rule too. It exists to stop emotional trading after a bad run. But you are not emotional; you are perfectly calm, and you can see the situation clearly. The rule was not written for this.

You stay in. You even find an elegant way to stay in: instead of selling, you buy a put spread against the position, so the loss is capped. It is a clever structure. It costs money, it caps the loss at a level well past where you were meant to be out, and it means you do not have to admit the trade is over.

By the close the position is closed, at a loss well past both rules. In the evening you replay the day, and you can explain every decision. Not one of them was stupid. Each was the kind of thought a smart trader has. Taken together, they were a disaster built entirely of good arguments.

---

*People are hard to govern because they have too much cleverness.* Laozi was talking about a state. A trader is a small state with one citizen, who also writes the laws, and who is very good at finding the gaps in them.

This is why intelligence does not protect traders from the oldest mistakes. It makes them more articulate about making them. The beginner who holds a loser knows he is hoping. The expert who holds a loser has a thesis.

---

The other way is to keep yourself plain during the hours the market is open.

The rule has no exceptions you can decide on while you are in a trade. Not because exceptions never exist, but because you are the worst judge of them when you have money on the screen. If you think a rule is wrong, you write that down and look at it at the weekend, with the market closed and nothing at stake. If it still looks wrong then, you change it, for every trade afterwards. Not for this one.

So when the price reaches the point, you get out. You notice the light volume, the important level below, the sector holding up, the news. You notice them and you get out anyway, the way a person with no clever thoughts would. If the stock turns at the important level an hour later, you were wrong in the small way that rules are sometimes wrong. The plain trader is wrong in small ways. The clever one is wrong in large ones.

It feels like giving something up. You worked hard to be able to see all of that. But your cleverness does not stop being useful. It moves to the evening and the weekend, where it designs the rules. It is kept out of the session, where it would only break them.

---

We are taught all our lives that the clever one wins. Laozi turns it over: in the place where you have to govern yourself, the plain one wins, because the plain one cannot talk himself out of anything. *To govern a state by cleverness is to rob it.* The thief is not the market. It is the voice that can always explain why the plan does not apply this time. The person who will have to follow your rules is the tired, frightened version of you at the worst moment of a bad day, and the rules have to be simple enough for him.

## Try This

For the next month, make every rule change on a weekend, in writing, with the market closed, and make none during a session. When you notice yourself building a reason to move a stop or skip a rule in a live trade, write the reason on a piece of paper, follow the rule anyway, and read the paper on Saturday.
