# 75. Why the People Starve

*Part II: The Virtue · 《民之饥》*

## The Verse

民之饥，以其上食税之多，是以饥。

民之难治，以其上之有为，是以难治。

民之轻死，以其求生之厚，是以轻死。夫唯无以生为者，是贤于贵生。

The people starve because those above them eat too much in taxes. That is why they starve.

The people are hard to govern because those above them are too busy. That is why they are hard to govern.

The people take death lightly because they reach too hard for life. That is why they take death lightly. Only one who does not make living his whole work is wiser than one who prizes life too much.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. An account starves the same way a people does: not from one bad harvest, but from what is taken off the top of every harvest.

2. The spread, the commission, the slippage, the fee on the data, the cost of being in the market one more time today. None of them is large. All of them are paid every time.

3. Count them once a year in a single line, next to the year's result. Most traders have never seen that line. It is not always pleasant to read.

4. A strategy whose edge is smaller than its costs is not a strategy. It is a way of paying someone else to watch you work.

5. The account is hard to govern because the one above it is too busy. Every extra trade is a tax you levy on yourself.

6. Activity feels like control. It is usually the opposite: the more you do, the less of the result is yours.

7. Reaching too hard for the result is how the result is lost. The trader who must make something this week trades more, sizes up, and pays the tax on all of it.

8. Before you add a trade, ask what it costs to be wrong and what it costs simply to be in. The second number is the one people forget.

9. Turning a strategy off is also a trade. It is often the cheapest one you will make all year.

10. Do not make trading your whole work. The one who can go a week without a ticket is wiser than the one who prizes every move.

## The Story

Once a year, in the dead week between Christmas and the new year, I close the books. Not the accounts. The notebooks. I read the year's log, the year's cards, the ledger of trials, and I write one page about what happened. For a long time that page had one line on it that mattered, the result, and a great many lines explaining it.

One year I added a second line underneath. It was a plain idea and I am a little embarrassed that it took me so long. Every cost the year had charged, added into a single number: the spreads crossed, the commissions, the slippage between the price I saw and the price I got, the fees for the data and the machines. Not estimated. Counted, trade by trade, from the fills.

It took most of an afternoon, because the costs did not live in one place. Some were on the statements. Some were in the fills, where the price I paid sat a little above the price on the screen when the order went out. Some were on invoices that came by email and went into a folder I never opened. Nobody had ever asked me to put them on one line. Nobody asks. The statement shows you what you made. It does not show you, in one number, what was taken off the top before you saw it.

---

When the line was done I looked at it for a long time.

It was not a disaster. No single item in it was large. That was the whole trouble. Every one of them was small enough to ignore on the day it was paid, and every one of them had been paid again and again, all year. *The people starve because those above them eat too much in taxes.* I had read that line as a line about emperors. It is a line about arithmetic. A tax does not have to be cruel to starve someone. It only has to be collected every time.

Then I broke the line down by strategy, and that is where the afternoon turned.

There were several rules running that year, and most of them traded rarely. Their share of the costs was what you would expect: a toll on each trip, and not many trips. One rule was different. It was a short-horizon rule, in and out within days, sometimes within the session, and it was the one I enjoyed most. It was busy. It gave me something to read every morning. On the board it was always doing something, and doing something felt like a sign of life.

Its share of the costs was most of the line.

I put its result beside its costs, and I will describe what I saw without giving you either number. Before costs, it had done something. After costs, what was left was too small to tell apart from luck. Most of the work it had done that year had been done for the people on the other side of the spread, and for the broker, who was paid in full on every trip whether the trip was any good or not. I had a strategy whose main product was commissions. I had been the busy ruler, and the account had paid the tax.

---

The obvious thing was to fix it. I could see the fixes immediately, the way you always can at a desk in December. Trade less often. Rest the orders instead of crossing the spread. Take only the strongest signals. Each one was reasonable, and I wrote them down.

But I did not put them into the rule. A rule changed is a different rule, with a different fingerprint, and it starts its count again at one. The fixes went into the ledger as a new trial, to be tested at the costs I had just counted, and then at twice those costs, the way everything is. If the quieter version was real, it would show it the slow way.

The old version I turned off. That took one line in a file and less than a minute.

I want to say something about that minute, because I had been avoiding it for longer than I like to admit. Turning a strategy off feels like a loss. It feels like admitting that the months were wasted, and like giving up the part of the morning I enjoyed. It is not a loss. It is a trade, and in this case the cheapest trade of the year, because it was the only one that paid no toll at all. Every morning after it, the account kept something it would otherwise have handed over before I was awake.

*The people are hard to govern because those above them are too busy.* The busy rule had not only taxed the account. It had taxed me. Half my reading each morning had gone to a rule that was mostly a channel through which money left. When it stopped, the board went quiet, and for a week the quiet felt like something broken. Then it felt like room.

---

The second line is on the page every year now. It sits directly under the result, the same size. I add it up before I write anything else, so that I cannot explain the year to myself without it.

And it went further than the page. Every forward book on the research desk now carries its costs as a column of its own, counted at the fill, beside the result. The random control book pays them too. The dice are charged the same toll on every trip, so that I can see what the costs alone do to a book that has no edge. It is a sobering column to read. It is the tax collector's view of my desk, and it never takes a day off.

*Only one who does not make living his whole work is wiser than one who prizes life too much.* The rule I enjoyed most was the one that needed most to be busy. That is the one I watch hardest now. I do not mean the rule that loses. I mean the one that is always doing something.
