# 75. Why the People Starve

*Part II: The Virtue · 《民之饥》*

## The Verse

民之饥，以其上食税之多，是以饥。

民之难治，以其上之有为，是以难治。

民之轻死，以其求生之厚，是以轻死。夫唯无以生为者，是贤于贵生。

The people starve because those above them eat too much in taxes. That is why they starve.

The people are hard to govern because those above them are too busy. That is why they are hard to govern.

The people take death lightly because they reach too hard for life. That is why they take death lightly. Only one who does not make living his whole work is wiser than one who prizes life too much.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

You were taught that busy people get ahead, and the market is no different. Commissions are close to nothing now, and the spread on a liquid name is a penny. A good trader is an active trader: the more setups you take, the more chances your edge has to show, and the faster you learn. Sitting on your hands is how you miss the move. If a trade has a fair chance, take it. The cost of being in is too small to matter; the cost of missing the runner is large. The traders who make real money are in the market every day, all day, and they never pass on an opportunity to save a few cents.

## The Other Way

1. *The people starve because those above them eat too much.* An account rarely dies of one bad trade. It thins from what is taken off the top of every trade.

2. The spread, the commission, the slippage on a market order in a fast tape, the data fee, the margin interest. None of them is large. Every one of them is paid every time.

3. You can see a loss. You cannot see a cost. It never gets a red line of its own; it hides inside every line.

4. A trade with a thin edge and a full cost is not a small win. It is a donation you make while feeling busy.

5. *Hard to govern because those above are too busy.* The account is unruly because you keep touching it. Every extra click is a tax you levy on yourself.

6. Activity feels like control. Most days, the more you do, the less of the result is yours.

7. *They take death lightly because they reach too hard for life.* The trader who must make money this week takes the trades he would skip in any other week, and pays full price for each.

8. Before a trade, ask what it costs to be wrong, and what it costs simply to be in. Nobody asks the second question.

9. The trade you did not take has no spread, no commission and no slippage. It is the cheapest decision you make all day.

10. *Only one who does not make living his whole work.* The trader whose whole life is not on the screen can let a day go by. Less, done this way, is more.

## The Story

It is a busy day, and you are good at busy days. From the open you are in and out of the same three names. A scalp on the first push, out for a few cents. A fade at the high, out at break-even. Back in on the pullback. Out when it stalls. In again when it reclaims the open. Each trade is small. Each one makes sense when you take it. By the close you have done thirty-odd round trips, and the P&L on the screen says you finished about flat.

Flat feels fine. The tape was choppy, you were sharp, you came out even. You call it a day of practice. Tomorrow the edge will show.

You do not look at what the day cost, because nothing on the screen asks you to. The number in the corner is the price you traded at, not the price you paid.

---

The month-end statement asks. It lists what the screen left out: the commissions, the exchange fees, the half-penny you gave up every time you hit the offer to get in quickly, the stops that filled a few cents worse than where you put them. One day of that is nothing. Twenty days of thirty round trips is a number you would not hand to a stranger for free. The days you called flat were not flat. They were small, steady withdrawals, and you made every one yourself. Nobody stole from you. The broker thanked you with fast fills.

Then ask why there were thirty trades. Not because there were thirty good setups. There were two, perhaps three. The rest were the week. The week had been slow, and you needed the day to be worth something, so every flicker on the chart looked like a chance. *They take death lightly because they reach too hard for life.* Reaching hard for a result, you took each trade lightly. That is how the cost got in. It always comes in through the trades you took lightly.

This is how most of us were taught to live, not only to trade. Fill the calendar. Answer every message. Never be caught doing nothing. A full day is a good day. Nobody counts what a full day takes off the top.

---

The next morning, before the open, you decide how many trades the day can afford. Say three. It is not a rule from a book; it is a budget, the way a household has one. Three, and you would rather spend two.

The open comes and pulls at you, as it always does. A name jumps and you feel the old reach. You ask the second question: what does it cost just to be in this? The spread is wide in the first minutes. The move is already half done. You let it go, and letting it go costs nothing. You notice that. It is the first free thing you have done in a while.

Mid-morning the setup you actually trust arrives. You use a limit order and accept that it may not fill. It fills. You manage it, you get out, and you sit back. There is a second one after lunch. By the close you have taken two trades, and you put the costs line next to the P&L. Both numbers are smaller than yesterday's. One of them is smaller by a great deal.

The day felt like you did not work. That feeling is worth noticing. It is the voice of the tax collector, and it has been speaking in your own voice for years.

---

Laozi was writing about rulers who ate the harvest and then blamed the people for being hungry. The trader is both: the one who levies the tax and the one who starves. The old Chinese view turns the busy man's lesson around. You were told that doing more gets you more. In a market where every action has a price, doing less keeps more.

At lunch you leave the desk and walk outside. The market goes on without you for an hour. Nothing is charged for that hour.

## Try This

For one week, decide before the open how many trades the day can afford, and write the number down. Keep it at half your usual count. At each close, put the day's costs next to the day's P&L and look at both. Watch what changes.
