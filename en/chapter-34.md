# 34. The Great Tao Flows Everywhere

*Part I: The Way · 《大道泛兮》*

## The Verse

大道泛兮，其可左右。

万物恃之而生而不辞，功成不名有。衣养万物而不为主，常无欲，可名于小；万物归焉而不为主，可名为大。

以其终不自为大，故能成其大。

The great way flows everywhere. It can go to the left or to the right.

The ten thousand things rely on it for life, and it does not turn them away. When the work is done, it does not claim it. It clothes and feeds the ten thousand things and does not act as their master. Always without desire, it can be called small. The ten thousand things return to it and it does not act as their master, so it can be called great.

Because it never makes itself great, it can become great.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

Believe in yourself. Confidence is half the battle. You heard it from coaches, before exams, before every job interview: own what you have done, take the credit, because nobody else will hand it to you. Trading needs it more than anything. A trader without confidence hesitates, and hesitation costs money. So you build confidence on your wins. When you have a strong month, that is the proof you are good, and you should let yourself feel it. You made those trades. They are yours. Carry that into the next session and trade like someone who knows what he is doing.

## The Other Way

1. *The great way flows everywhere. It can go to the left or to the right.* The market moved, and you happened to be standing where it went. Some of that was your doing. A good deal of it was not.

2. *When the work is done, it does not claim it.* After a winning trade, the useful question is not how good you are. It is what the market did.

3. Claimed credit comes with a bill. Once the wins are yours, the losses have to be yours too, and you will start to look for ways to make them someone else's.

4. Confidence built on wins rises with the account and falls with it. It is weather, and you are trying to stand on it.

5. *It does not act as their master.* After a run of winners you begin to feel that you are steering the market. That feeling is the moment size begins to grow.

6. *Always without desire, it can be called small.* Stay small in your own eyes and your size tends to stay small too. The trader who feels great trades great size.

7. The worst loss of a season usually comes after the best week, not the worst one. The best week is when you stop checking.

8. *The ten thousand things rely on it, and it does not turn them away.* The trade after a big win gets the same questions, the same checklist and the same size as the trade after a loss.

9. The confidence a trader needs is small and plain: enough to follow the plan. It does not depend on whether you are good.

10. *Because it never makes itself great, it can become great.* The trader who never decides he has made it keeps doing the small things that got him there.

## The Story

It has been three good weeks. Seven winners out of eight trades, and the eighth was a scratch. The market has been rising steadily, and you have been long, at the right levels, out at sensible places. You look at the account on Friday evening and something settles in you: you have figured it out. You tell a friend over dinner. You do not say it as a boast, but you hear it come out that way.

Monday you sit down differently. You can feel it in your shoulders. You skip the morning list you have run every day for a year, because you know all this now. A stock is moving early, not quite your usual kind of setup, and you take it at twice your normal size. You have earned that. Your reads have been right for three weeks.

At ten it turns against you. It reaches the price where you would normally be out. You do not get out. You have been right too often lately to be wrong this quickly, and the market has been going your way. You give it room. By noon it has taken half of three weeks.

Tuesday is the other side of the same coin. The confidence has gone as fast as it came. A clean setup appears, exactly your kind, at your level, and you freeze. You are not sure you know anything anymore. It runs without you. On Monday you were too large and on Tuesday you were absent, and both days the account was making the decisions while you thought they were yours.

---

The same three weeks. Each evening after a winner, before you write a word about what you did, you write one line about what the market did. Most of the lines are plain: *the index rose all day; long the whole way. The sector was bid from the open.* A few say: *followed the plan, got out where I said.* That is all the credit you take.

Friday you look at the account and feel good. You let yourself feel it. Then you look at the lines, and the feeling sits in a more modest place.

Monday you run the morning list, the same as every day. The early mover is not your kind of setup, and you notice that you want it anyway, and you notice why. You pass. Later a setup of your own appears and you take it at your usual size. It goes against you and you are out at your price, an ordinary loss, the kind you take every other week.

Tuesday the clean setup appears. You take it at your usual size, because nothing about you changed on Monday either. You were not great on Friday and you are not useless now. You are the same person running the same list.

---

We are taught to believe in ourselves and to own our successes, because they are proof of what we are worth. The old book sees it the other way round. The way feeds everything and claims nothing, and *because it never makes itself great, it can become great.*

The three good weeks belonged mostly to the market. The size on Monday would have belonged to you.

## Try This

For the next two weeks, after every winning trade, write one line naming what the market did that made it work, before you write anything about what you did. For the three sessions after your best day, keep your size exactly where it was and run your usual checks in full. At the end, read the lines and count how many of the wins would have come to almost anyone holding the same position.
