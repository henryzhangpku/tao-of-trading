# Sources

*The texts this book quotes, the translations it uses, the books it owes a debt to, and the first line of every chapter of the* Tao Te Ching.

## The Chinese text

**The received Wang Bi text.** Every verse in this book is quoted from the received text of the *Tao Te Ching* (《道德经》, also called the *Laozi* 《老子》) as transmitted with the commentary of Wang Bi 王弼 (226–249), 《老子道德经注》. It is the text that most Chinese readers grew up with and the one most modern editions print. It is in the public domain. The Chinese is given in simplified characters; a traditional-character edition can be generated from the same text.

The Wang Bi text is not the only one. The silk manuscripts found at Mawangdui 马王堆 in 1973 and the bamboo slips found at Guodian 郭店 in 1993 are older, differ in many small readings, and in the silk manuscripts the second part, on virtue 德, comes before the first, on the way 道. Where a reading in those manuscripts would change the sense of a line that a chapter of this book depends on, the chapter says so. Otherwise this book keeps to the received text and its order of eighty-one chapters, because that is the book a reader can buy in any shop in China and check line by line.

The received text also has small variant readings among its printed editions. Where one matters to a chapter, the chapter notes it. Two that readers may meet in the table below: chapter 31 opens 夫佳兵者 in the received Wang Bi text and 夫兵者 in several other editions, and this book's chapter title uses the shorter reading; chapter 34 is printed both 大道泛兮 and 大道氾兮, with the same meaning.

## Translations

**The author's own.** Unless a chapter marks a line otherwise, every English translation in this book is the author's own, made from the Chinese. It aims to be plain and close rather than beautiful, and where a word has two senses it chooses the one the chapter needs and does not pretend the other is absent. Chapter 81 says why.

**James Legge, 1891.** Where a line is borrowed, it is from James Legge, *The Sacred Books of China: The Texts of Taoism*, Part I, in *The Sacred Books of the East*, vol. 39, edited by F. Max Müller (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1891). It is in the public domain. A borrowed line is set in italics and the chapter's source note names Legge. The first such line is in chapter 64: *the journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step.*

The Chinese edition of this book quotes the original in full and gives a plain modern Chinese rendering (白话今译) of its own; it borrows no one's.

## Other classical lines this book quotes

- **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*** (《孙子兵法》). 以迂为直, "make the roundabout route the direct one," is from chapter 7, 军争; 势 *shi* has its own chapter, 5, 兵势. Neither line is Laozi's, and the glossary says so. The author's companion book, *The Art of Options War*, follows Sun Tzu chapter by chapter.
- **Confucius, *Analects*** (《论语》) 13.17, 子路: 欲速则不达, "haste does not arrive." Often attributed to Laozi; it is not his. The prologue says so once.

## The book this one owes a debt to

**Mark Spitznagel, *The Dao of Capital: Austrian Investing in a Distorted World*** (Hoboken, NJ: Wiley, 2013). A touchstone for this book's tone and its subject: the patient, indirect route over the direct grab, 势 *shi* and 迂 the roundabout path, read for the investor across years and decades. This book credits it where the ideas meet (the prologue; the afterword, *Trading, Not Investing*; and chapters 22, 36 and 40) and quotes none of its text. Its readings are its own; the readings of Laozi here are this author's, made from the Chinese.

## The author's own books referred to

- *The Art of Options War* · 《期权兵法》. Sun Tzu's thirteen chapters for the options trader. Open edition, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.
- *The Counted Trade* · 《算而后战》 and *The Options Underwriter* · 《期权承保》, the companion volumes.

No other book's text is quoted. Where an idea in a chapter came from someone else, the chapter names them.
