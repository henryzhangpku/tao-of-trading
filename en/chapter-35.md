# 35. Hold the Great Image

*Part I: The Way · 《执大象》*

## The Verse

执大象，天下往。往而不害，安平太。

乐与饵，过客止。道之出口，淡乎其无味，视之不足见，听之不足闻，用之不足既。

Hold the great image, and the world comes to you. It comes and comes to no harm: safety, peace and calm.

Music and good food make the passing traveller stop. But the way, when it leaves the mouth, is bland and has no flavour. Look at it, and there is not enough to see. Listen to it, and there is not enough to hear. Use it, and it cannot be used up.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. Good rules are boring to describe. If your best rule makes the table lean forward at dinner, test it again.

2. Music and bait make the traveller stop: the screenshot, the story, the month that changed everything. The way has no flavour.

3. Bland is a feature. A rule with no story attached gives you nothing to fall in love with, and nothing to defend when it is wrong.

4. Look at it, and there is not enough to see: an entry, a size, a stop, an exit. Four lines on a card.

5. Listen to it, and there is not enough to hear. A system doing its job makes very little noise.

6. Use it, and it cannot be used up. The bland rule does not need a new market, a new story or a new season to keep working the way it works.

7. Hold the great image, and the world comes. Hold the plain rule, and the trades arrive on their own schedule, not yours.

8. It comes to no harm. A process that is safe to run every day is worth more than one that is thrilling to run once.

9. If you explain your rule and the table loses interest, you have probably explained it correctly.

## The Story

It was a family dinner, the long kind, at a round table with a lazy Susan in the middle and more dishes than anyone could finish. Somewhere between the fish and the soup, one of my uncles was telling the table about a stock.

He told it well. He had heard about the company from a friend's son, who worked somewhere near it. He had bought before anyone else had heard of it. There had been a terrible week when he nearly sold, and he had not sold, and afterwards the thing had gone so far that he still said the number in a lowered voice. The table leaned in. Someone refilled his cup without being asked. It was a good story. It had music and it had bait: a secret, a test of nerve, a reward.

Then somebody turned to me. You build those trading machines, they said. What is your best one? What does it do?

---

I have been asked that many times, and I know there are two ways to answer.

One is to tell a story like my uncle's: the week a rule caught a move, the morning a refusal kept me out of something that later fell apart. I have a few of those. They are true, and they would have held the table for the length of a dish. The trouble is what they would have been. Each is one trade picked out of a great many because it makes a good story, which is the very thing the rule exists not to do. If I told the table my best trade, I would be telling it what my rule is not.

The other way is to describe the rule.

So I described it. It looks for markets already moving in one direction. It buys an amount sized so that the same share of the account is at risk on every trade. It puts the stop a set distance away, measured in the market's own recent swings. It leaves when the move reverses past a shorter line, or after a set number of days if nothing has happened. It refuses markets too thin to leave. That is all.

I watched the table while I said it. It took about as long as the fish takes to go once round the lazy Susan. By the stop, an aunt was looking at the soup. By the exit, my uncle was explaining to someone else how his friend's son had heard about the company in the first place.

---

One young relative stayed with me, out of politeness, I think. "But how do you know it works?"

I gave the truest answer I have, and it was even more boring than the rule. I said that I do not know yet, not in the way the question meant. The rule is frozen, pinned by a fingerprint, so that nobody, including me, can change it while it runs. It is being tested forward, on prices that did not exist when it was written, for a season. Every day it is compared with a book of the same size chosen by dice, so that a good market cannot pass itself off as a good rule. And every version of it I ever tried is written in a ledger, the failed ones included, so that I cannot forget how many times I looked.

The nod I got was the nod people give to a description of a filing system. Then the question went across the table to my uncle: what was the company called?

---

On the drive home I noticed something about myself, and it is the reason this story is in this chapter. While I was describing the rule I had felt, twice, the urge to make it more interesting. Once to add the good week. Once to say something about what it might catch next. Neither would have been a lie, exactly. Both would have been bait. And the urge had not come from the rule. The rule has nothing to say about next week. It came from the table, and from the part of me that wanted the table back.

That is the danger in a beautiful description. It does not stay at dinner. A rule you have made exciting to other people is a rule you will want to keep exciting, and the way to keep it exciting is to change it: add the filter that would have caught last month's move, lean towards the story that held the room. The bland rule has nothing in it to defend in public. That is part of why it can be left alone.

*The way, when it leaves the mouth, is bland and has no flavour.* I used to hear that line as a complaint, the way you would say a dish needs salt. Now I hear it as a test. If I describe a rule and the table leans in, I have probably described a story about the rule. If they go back to the soup, I have probably described the rule.

*Use it, and it cannot be used up.* My uncle's story will be told again, and it will get better each time, the way stories do. The rule will be the same few lines tomorrow. Nobody at that table will ask me about it again, and I take that as a good sign.
