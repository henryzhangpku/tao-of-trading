# 56. Those Who Know Do Not Speak

*Part II: The Virtue · 《知者不言》*

## The Verse

知者不言，言者不知。

塞其兑，闭其门，挫其锐，解其纷，和其光，同其尘，是谓玄同。

故不可得而亲，不可得而疏；不可得而利，不可得而害；不可得而贵，不可得而贱。故为天下贵。

Those who know do not speak. Those who speak do not know.

Block the openings. Shut the doors. Blunt the sharp, untie the tangled, soften the glare, settle with the dust. This is called the dark sameness.

So he cannot be drawn close and cannot be pushed away. He cannot be helped and cannot be harmed. He cannot be honoured and cannot be shamed. That is why the world honours him.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

Say it out loud and you will follow through. You were taught that a goal you tell people is a goal you keep: announce the diet, tell your friends about the marathon, put the plan in front of the team. Accountability works. Trading is lonely, so traders need it more than anyone. Post your trades. Share your levels in the group. Talk your ideas through with people who will push back. If you have to explain a trade, you will think it through first, and if people are watching, you will not cheat on your stop. Openness is a sign you have nothing to hide.

## The Other Way

1. *Those who know do not speak.* A trade has one job: to be taken and closed at the right prices. The moment you tell it to someone, it gets a second job. It has to make you look right.

2. Once said, a position has an audience. The audience sets its own stop, at the price where you would look foolish, and that price is never the one you wrote down.

3. *Those who speak do not know.* The loudest calls in any room are made by people who are not counted against them. Listen to them long enough and you start talking like them.

4. *Block the openings. Shut the doors.* Keep open positions off the group chat, off the feed, off the dinner table. Talk about trades when they are closed, if at all.

5. Explaining a trade feels like testing it. Mostly it is rehearsing it. Each time you tell the story it gets smoother, and a smooth story is harder to sell.

6. *It blunts the sharp.* An idea held quietly stays soft. You can drop it without cost. An idea you have defended in public has grown a point, and it points at you.

7. *He cannot be honoured and cannot be shamed.* If no one knows what you hold, no one can praise you for holding it or laugh at you for selling it. Your exit is free again.

8. Praise is a kind of position. When people cheered your last call, you started trading to be cheered again.

9. *He cannot be drawn close and cannot be pushed away.* The quiet trader is not cold. He simply has nothing in the conversation to protect.

10. Accountability is real. Give it to the page, not the crowd. A notebook remembers your stop and does not care how you look.

## The Story

It is Monday morning and you have an idea you like. You did the work over the weekend: the chart, the level, the reason. You buy at the open. Then, because it is a good idea and you are proud of it, you post it in the group: entry, target, stop. A few people react. One says nice. Someone you respect says he is in too.

Now the trade is not quite yours.

---

By eleven it has moved your way. You post an update. More reactions. You feel it in your chest, a small warm pressure, and you check the group more often than the chart. After lunch it gives the gain back and slips below your entry. The stop is still a fair way off. You do not post. You notice that you do not post.

At two it trades down to your stop. In your notebook the stop is a price. In your head it has become a sentence you will have to type: *stopped out.* The person who followed you in is still in. You picture the silence after the message. So you move the stop down. You tell yourself it is because the level was a little tight anyway, and you half believe it.

It goes lower. Now it is not a loss; it is a loss you have told people about. You start explaining it to them in your head, and then in the group: the thesis is intact, this is noise, longer time frame. Each message makes it harder to sell. You hold it overnight. You hold it into Wednesday. When you finally sell, the loss is three times the one you planned, and you do it without posting, which is its own small shame.

What went wrong was not the idea. Ideas fail all the time. What went wrong was that you sold the trade twice: once to the market, and once to the room. The room would not let you take it back.

---

Now the other Monday.

Same idea, same work, same open. You buy. You write the entry, the stop and the reason in your notebook. You do not post it. The urge to post is surprisingly strong. You want someone to see that you saw it. You notice the want and close the chat window.

By eleven it moves your way. Nobody knows. It feels oddly flat, a gain with no applause. After lunch it gives the gain back and slips below your entry. You look at the notebook. The stop is where you wrote it.

At two it trades down to the stop. You sell. It is a small, ordinary loss, the size you agreed to on Sunday. There is no sentence to type, no follower to face, no story to keep alive. You write one line under the trade: *stopped, as planned.* Then you look at the rest of the market, which you can see again.

On Friday, if someone asks, you can say you had a loss on Monday. It will take five words and nobody will care. That is how it should be.

---

*Those who know do not speak.* We were taught that saying it makes it real, and that openness keeps you honest. The old book sees it the other way round. Speaking ties you to the image of yourself you just made, and in trading that image has to be sold at a bad price. *He cannot be honoured and cannot be shamed.* The quiet trader keeps his exit free.

Nobody will know about your best trade this month. You will have to be fine with that.

## Try This

For the next two weeks, tell no one about any position while it is open: no posts, no messages, no mention at dinner. Write each trade in your own notebook instead, with the stop and one line of reason. If the urge to share comes, write down who you wanted to tell and why. At the end of the two weeks, look at your exits: whether you sold where you wrote, and how often the urge to share came just before you wanted to move a stop.
