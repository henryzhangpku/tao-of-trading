# 68. The Good Warrior Is Not Warlike

*Part II: The Virtue · 《善为士者不武》*

## The Verse

善为士者不武，善战者不怒，善胜敌者不与，善用人者为之下。

是谓不争之德，是谓用人之力，是谓配天，古之极。

The good officer is not warlike. The good fighter is not angry. The one good at defeating an enemy does not engage him. The one good at using people places himself beneath them.

This is called the virtue of not contending. This is called the strength of using others. This is called matching heaven: the highest of the ancients.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

Trading is a fight, and you fight to win. It is what every coach told you: hate losing, and never let anyone push you around. When the market takes money from you, you get it back. Anger is not the enemy; it is fuel. The traders who make it are the ones who refuse to accept a loss. A stop-out is a challenge, and you answer it. You hit back, you press, you show the market it cannot shake you. A trader who just shrugs at a loss has stopped caring, and a trader who has stopped caring has stopped getting better.

## The Other Way

1. *The good officer is not warlike.* You do not need to look like a fighter to trade well. The trader who looks fiercest is often the one losing.

2. *The good fighter is not angry.* Anger narrows the eye to one ticker and one number: what you lost.

3. Anger does not know the price. It only knows how much it wants back.

4. *The one good at defeating an enemy does not engage him.* The market is not your opponent. You cannot hurt it. It does not know you were stopped out.

5. A revenge trade has a shape: bigger, faster, in the same name, straight after the loss. You can recognise one before you place it.

6. Do not engage. After a loss that stings, the best trade is often no trade until the sting has gone.

7. *The strength of using others.* Let the market's own force carry the trade. Join a move that already exists, at a place it has already shown you. Do not push against it to prove you were right.

8. *Places himself beneath them.* Take the lower seat: the market leads and you follow. It is the only seat that pays.

9. *The virtue of not contending.* Not fighting is not giving up. It is refusing a fight you cannot win and do not need. The one who does not fight is the one who is not beaten.

10. *Matching heaven.* Heaven is not angry at the storm. Take the loss the way weather is taken: it happened, you are wet, you go inside.

## The Story

You are stopped out at ten in the morning. A clean stop, at the price you named, a normal loss. A minute later the stock turns and runs straight back through your entry and on up without you.

You know this feeling in the jaw and the hands. It is not disappointment. It is anger, and it has an object. The market took you out on the low tick. Someone ran your stop. You were right and you were robbed.

So you buy it back. Higher than you sold it, with more size, because now there is a loss to make up as well as a trade to make. You do not write down where you would be wrong this time. You were "wrong" there ten minutes ago, and you were right. The stock pulls back. You hold. It pulls back further. You add, because you are not going to be shaken out twice. By the time you sell, the second loss is three times the first, and you have spent the whole hour fighting someone who was not there.

That is the part to see. The stop-out was not personal. No one in the market knew your price. The run afterwards was not aimed at you either. You turned an ordinary loss into an enemy, went to war with it, and the war cost more than the loss.

The anger felt like caring. It felt like the opposite of the shrug the books warn you about. But caring about the money would have meant protecting it, and the anger spent it. What the anger cared about was being right, and the market had no interest in that.

---

*The good fighter is not angry.* Laozi does not say the good fighter never feels anger. He says the good fighter does not fight from it.

Same morning. Stopped out at ten, normal loss. The stock turns and runs back through your entry. You feel the same heat in the jaw. This time you notice it, and you name it to yourself: *angry*. That is all. You do not argue with it. You take your hand off the mouse.

Then you do the thing that is hardest when you are angry: nothing, for a while. You stand up. You walk to the window or the kitchen. Five minutes, ten. Anger does not live long if you do not feed it. When you sit back down, the stock is still there, and so is the market. Neither noticed you were gone.

Now you can look at it as a trade and not a grudge. Is there a setup here? Maybe it has pulled back to a place where buyers have shown up before, and you can buy it at your normal size with a level you can name. That is using the market's strength instead of your own: you join a move that already exists. If there is no such place, you let it go. It is one stock on one morning. There will be others, and none of them will owe you anything.

---

*The one good at defeating an enemy does not engage him.* The trader who refuses the fight loses only what the stop cost. The trader who takes up the fight loses what the stop cost and then whatever the fight costs, and nobody sets a limit on that in advance.

It is the same away from the screen. The argument you walk out of costs you an evening. The one you insist on winning can cost you a friend. The market is easier than people in one way: it never wanted the fight in the first place.

## Try This

For the next two weeks, keep one rule by the screen: after a loss that makes you angry, no new trade in that name for the rest of the hour. When the anger comes, say the word to yourself and stand up. Watch what the hour costs you, and what it saves.
