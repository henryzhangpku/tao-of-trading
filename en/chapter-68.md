# 68. The Good Warrior Is Not Warlike

*Part II: The Virtue · 《善为士者不武》*

## The Verse

善为士者不武，善战者不怒，善胜敌者不与，善用人者为之下。

是谓不争之德，是谓用人之力，是谓配天，古之极。

The good officer is not warlike. The good fighter is not angry. The one good at defeating an enemy does not engage him. The one good at using people places himself beneath them.

This is called the virtue of not contending. This is called the strength of using others. This is called matching heaven: the highest of the ancients.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. The good trader is not warlike. The market is not an enemy, and a trade is not a fight. A trader who comes to the screen to fight will find one every morning.

2. The good fighter is not angry. Anger is a size multiplier. It doubles the position and halves the time you are willing to wait.

3. After a loss, the hand wants the next trade to repair the last one. That trade has no plan behind it, only a debt. It is a position with no stop, because you are sure, and no reason, except that you are owed.

4. The market does not know it took your money. There is no one to get even with. The revenge trade is aimed at an empty chair.

5. The one good at defeating an enemy does not engage him. When the same name that stopped you out turns and runs back through your entry, you do not have to go back in. Not engaging is not losing.

6. Write the rule before the loss: the next trade after a stopped-out trade waits for the next session. A rule like that cannot be written on the day it is needed. On that day it can only be obeyed.

7. If the losses come in a run, step further back: to paper, or to a size too small to hurt, until you can read your own trades without wincing.

8. The one good at using people places himself beneath them. Put yourself beneath your rules. The rule is the senior partner; you are the one who carries out its orders, especially on the day you would like to overrule it.

9. The virtue of not contending: you cannot lose an argument you do not start with the market.

10. Matching heaven. Heaven is not angry at anyone. Neither is a good stop.

## The Story

The stop was hit at twenty to eleven in the morning, at the price I had written on the plan the night before. It was not a disaster. It was the size the rule allows, cut where the rule cuts it, and the loss was the loss I had agreed to before I entered. On paper there was nothing to feel.

By a quarter to twelve the name had come back up through my entry price and kept going.

---

If you have traded for any time you know the feeling, and if you have not, I am not sure I can describe it. It is not quite regret. It is closer to being robbed by someone who then stands across the street and waves at you. The stop had done its job, and the market had taken it, and now the market was going where I had said it would go, without me.

Every part of me wanted to go back in. And the part that frightens me, looking back, is that the trade I wanted to make was not the trade I had planned. It was larger. I had already done the arithmetic in my head: if I bought twice the size here, I would be back to even by the close. That is not a plan. That is a sentence with a debt in it. There was no stop in that trade, because I was certain now, and the certainty had been supplied by the loss.

*The good fighter is not angry.* I was angry. Not loudly, but enough that I noticed my jaw.

---

The rule is simple. After a stop is hit, there is no new entry in that name until the next session. Not a lower size, not a better setup, not a quick scalp to win back the spread. The next session. In the system I trade with, the rule is not a note on the wall. It is a check in the code. When a stop fills, the name is marked, and until the next session begins, any entry in it is refused with a plain reason in the log.

I opened the order screen anyway. I want to be truthful about that. I opened it to see whether it would really refuse me, the way you test a locked door you yourself locked. It refused me. The reason was one line, in words I had written, which is a strange thing to be told by: *cooling period after stop, next session*.

*The one good at using people places himself beneath them.* I had built the rule so that on that morning I would be beneath it. It is the only kind of discipline I fully trust: the kind decided by a calmer person, earlier, and carried out by a machine that does not know I am upset.

---

The desk I work at has a language model that reads the day's activity and proposes things. It is allowed to propose anything; it is never allowed to place a trade. That afternoon, reading the same price action I had read, it proposed a re-entry in the same name. Its reasons were tidy: the stop had been taken out by a brief dip, the trend had resumed, the setup looked valid again.

The model was not angry. It had no jaw to clench. Its proposal was, as far as I could tell, about as good as mine would have been, and made without any of the feeling. And the rule refused it in exactly the same words.

That was the moment I understood what the rule was for. I had always thought of it as a guard against my emotion, and it is. But it is also a guard against a reason. After a loss, a good reason to go straight back in is always available, from a model or from a man, and the reason being good is not the point. The point is that the trade is being made in the shadow of the last one. The rule does not ask whether the re-entry is clever. It asks what time it is.

---

The next morning, before the open, I wrote up the trade. I write up every stopped-out trade the same way: why I entered, where the stop was, whether the stop was where the plan said, whether I moved it. All four answers were clean. The entry was by the rule, the stop was by the rule, it was hit, it was honoured. As a piece of work, it was a good trade that lost.

Then I wrote up the trade I had not made, the one I had wanted at a quarter to twelve: twice the size, no stop, entered to get even. I wrote it as if I had made it, with the size and the reason, so that I could look at it in daylight. The reason, written down, was *I was owed*. There is no rule in any book that would take a trade for that reason. Reading it the next morning, I could not quite believe I had wanted it.

---

I would like to say that the name came back down and the rule was proved right. I do not remember what it did over the following days, and I have decided not to look. If it went higher, the rule cost me a trade I might have won. If it fell, the rule saved me from one I might have lost. Either way the rule is the same rule, and judging it by one name on one day is exactly the kind of thinking it exists to prevent.

What I can say is what the rule has given me since, which is not a result but a kind of morning. A stop is hit, and the rest of the day in that name is decided. There is nothing to fight about. The anger, when it comes, has nowhere to go, and after a while it stops coming as hard.

The next session came. The name was marked as clear. I looked at it, found that it did not meet the rule, and passed.
