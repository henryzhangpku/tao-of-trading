# 29. The World Cannot Be Forced

*Part I: The Way · 《将欲取天下而为之》*

## The Verse

将欲取天下而为之，吾见其不得已。天下神器，不可为也。为者败之，执者失之。

故物或行或随，或歔或吹，或强或羸，或挫或隳。是以圣人去甚，去奢，去泰。

Whoever wants to take the world and work on it: I see that he will not get there. The world is a sacred vessel. It cannot be worked on. Whoever works on it ruins it. Whoever grips it loses it.

So among things some lead and some follow, some breathe gently and some blow hard, some are strong and some are frail, some are broken and some fall. So the sage puts away the extreme, the extravagant and the excessive.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. You cannot make a trade work. You can only decide what it is allowed to cost.

2. The market is a sacred vessel: not holy, only not yours. Whoever works on it ruins it.

3. Every way of forcing a trade looks like effort. Adding to it. Moving the stop. Giving it one more day. Explaining it again, to yourself, with more conviction.

4. Whoever grips it loses it. Averaging down is gripping with both hands.

5. Some lead and some follow, some breathe gently and some blow hard. You do not choose which kind of market you are in. You choose whether to be in it.

6. Strong and frail, broken and fallen: every position is each of these on some day. Size it for the day it is frail.

7. Put away the extreme: the size that would matter if you were wrong.

8. Put away the extravagant: the trade taken because the account can afford it, not because the rule asked for it.

9. Put away the excessive: the position that was already enough, made more.

10. A losing position gets smaller or it gets closed. It never gets bigger.

## The Story

Most of the rules I trade by were written by someone who had just lost something. This one was written by me, on a Saturday, years ago, at the kitchen table, in pencil.

---

It started the way these things start, with a company I thought I understood. I had read everything about it. I had a view of the next two years, and the view was good. I bought a position of ordinary size, and within a week it was lower, on no news I could find.

I bought more. The reasoning felt like research paying off. If it had been worth buying higher, it was worth more of it lower. Better price, same company. People I admired said this sort of thing in interviews, and I had read the interviews.

It went lower. I bought more again.

I want to describe what that felt like, because it did not feel like a mistake. It felt like conviction. Each purchase came with a sentence, and each sentence was stronger than the last, because each one had to carry more. By the end of the month it was the largest position in the account. I had never decided that it should be. Each falling day had decided a little of it for me.

---

The arithmetic is the seductive part, so it is worth doing once.

Buy one unit at 100. It falls to 90. Buy a second unit there. You now own two units at an average of 95, and the price only needs to get back to 95, not 100, for you to stand still. The bar has come down. That is the whole appeal, and it is real.

What it hides is the other side. The first fall of 10 cost you 10. The next fall of 10 costs you 20, because you own twice as much. The bar has come down and the floor has moved away, at the same time, with the same order. Every add makes the price you need closer and the price that hurts you more dangerous. Do it three times and a move that would once have been a bad week is now a bad year.

*Whoever works on it ruins it.* I was not trading that stock any more. I was working on it, as if owning more of it would make it come back sooner. The market did not know I was there, and it did not care how much I owned.

---

There was no crash at the end. That is what I remember most clearly. One morning the stock opened a little lower again, an ordinary amount, and I noticed that I had checked it on my phone while standing in line for coffee, before I had looked at anything else. I had stopped having an account with many things in it. I had one thing, and it had me.

I sold all of it at the open. Not because of the price. Because of the phone in the coffee line.

I did not feel relief. I felt foolish, and then, an hour later, something stranger: light. The account was smaller than it had been, and I had my whole attention back.

I am not going to tell you whether the stock came back. It does not matter, and that is not a dodge. Plenty of names come back after you sell them. The ones that do not come back are the ones that end accounts, and on the morning you are holding one you cannot tell which kind it is. A rule that only works when the stock comes back is not a rule. It is a hope with a cost basis.

---

That Saturday I wrote one line at the top of a new page.

*No order may increase a losing position.*

Not "should not." May not. I wrote underneath it the reasons I knew I would give myself later, so that I would recognise them when they came: the better price, the same company, the long-term view, the one more time. Each of them had sounded like wisdom while I was doing it. On paper, in a row, they looked like what they were: the same sentence, said louder.

It took some years before that line was more than pencil. Today it is a check that runs before any order goes out. If an order would add to a position that is below its entry, it is refused and the refusal is written to the log, with the time and the name. There is no setting to switch it off for one trade. I did that on purpose. The person most likely to want it switched off is me, on the day it matters.

Adding is still allowed. It is allowed to a position that is working, when the rule that opened it says so, and even then it is a separate, deliberate act, made on one screen. Never to a loser. A losing position gets smaller or it gets closed.

---

Not long ago I was reading the night's log before five, the way I do, and found that line in it. *Refused: add to losing position.* A name in the book had fallen hard the previous afternoon, and a model on the desk, asked for ideas, had suggested that the lower price was a better entry. The suggestion had become an order request, and the order request had met the rule.

I laughed, alone in the kitchen. The model's reasoning was the same reasoning I had used at that table years ago, almost word for word: better price, same story. It was not a stupid suggestion. It was a human one. The rule I had written on my worst Saturday had just turned it down without needing to argue.

*So the sage puts away the extreme, the extravagant and the excessive.* I used to read that as a line about restraint, a virtue you practise. I read it now as a line about construction. You put the extreme away by building something that will not let it in. The world goes where it goes. The sage does not stop it. He stops himself from going after it with more.
