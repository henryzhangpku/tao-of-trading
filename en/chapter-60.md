# 60. Cooking a Small Fish

*Part II: The Virtue · 《治大国，若烹小鲜》*

## The Verse

治大国，若烹小鲜。

以道莅天下，其鬼不神；非其鬼不神，其神不伤人；非其神不伤人，圣人亦不伤人。

夫两不相伤，故德交归焉。

Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish.

When the Tao presides over the world, its ghosts lose their power. It is not that the ghosts lose their power; it is that their power does not harm people. It is not only that their power does not harm people; the sage does not harm them either.

When neither harms the other, virtue flows back to both.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## What You Know

A position is not something you put on and forget. Markets change by the minute, and a good trader changes with them. When a trade moves your way, you tighten the stop to protect it. When it stalls, you trim. When it pulls back, you add, or you get out and get back in lower. Every tick is information, and a trader who ignores information is lazy. It is the same everywhere: the people who care are the ones who stay hands-on. The best traders are always managing. Sitting still with a position is how you give money back.

## The Other Way

1. *Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish.* Turn a small fish too often and it falls apart in the pan. A trade is the same.

2. Every adjustment feels like care. Most of them are a nervous hand.

3. Decide the trade before you enter: the size, the place you are wrong, the place you would be content. Then the cooking is mostly waiting.

4. Each stop moved closer because you are afraid is a new trade with a worse plan, entered without noticing.

5. Trimming a winner at the first wobble and adding back at the next is two trades, two spreads, and no view.

6. *Its ghosts lose their power.* The tick against you, the sudden spike, the headline: they are ghosts. They have power only over the hand that reaches for the ticket.

7. *Their power does not harm people.* Noise cannot hurt a position whose stop is already where it belongs.

8. *The sage does not harm them either.* The market is not the only one who can wreck your trade. You can, with your own fingers.

9. Change the plan when the reason for the trade is gone, not when your feeling about it changes.

10. *When neither harms the other, virtue flows back to both.* Leave the trade alone and the trade leaves you alone.

## The Story

You get into a good trade in the first hour. It is the setup you trust, at the size you meant, with your stop where it should be. Within ten minutes it is up nicely.

Then you start cooking.

The first pullback comes and you move the stop up to break-even, because a winner must never become a loser. That sounds like a rule. It was not in your plan. A few minutes later the price dips, touches your new stop by a cent, and you are out at break-even. Then it turns and goes back up without you.

You buy it back, higher. Smaller this time, because you are annoyed. It runs. You add, because now you are right again and you are under-sized. It stalls. You trim half, because it is stalling. It goes up again. You add back the half, at a worse price. Near the close it slips, and you sell everything because you do not want to carry it overnight, although you had planned to.

In the evening you count. Seven orders on one idea. The idea was right all day. The stock closed well above where you first bought it. You made a little, less than the first ten minutes showed you, and you paid the spread seven times.

---

Nobody calls this a mistake. Nobody even calls it overtrading. It is called managing the position, and traders are proud of it. Each decision had a reason, and each reason was made of the last few minutes of price.

That is the trouble. The trade was planned on a scale of days. You managed it on a scale of minutes. Every flip of the fish tore a little more off it.

Laozi chose the smallest thing in the kitchen to talk about the largest thing in the world. A great state, he says, is ruined by the same thing that ruins a small fish: too much handling. The cook who means well is the danger. The less the hand does, the more the dish holds together.

---

The other way starts before the order goes in.

You decide three things: the size, the price where the trade is wrong, and the place, or the time, where you would be glad to be done. You write them down. Then you enter.

The first pullback comes. You look at your note. The price is above the place where you are wrong. There is nothing to do. The ghosts come: a sharp spike down, a headline, someone in a chat room saying the market is about to break. You notice them. You look at the note again. They have no power over a trade whose plan already accounts for them.

If you need something to do with your hands, stand up. Get water. Put the chart on a slower timeframe, the one the trade was planned on. On a daily chart, the morning's frightening dip is a small wrinkle.

Sometimes the trade hits your stop. Then you are out at the price you chose, which is how a trade is supposed to fail. Sometimes it reaches the place you named. Then you are done, and you take it, and you do not stay to see if there was more.

Most days it does neither. It drifts, and the close comes, and you still hold it as you planned. That is the whole day. One order in, nothing in between.

---

There is a real reason to change a plan: the reason for the trade is gone. The thing you were trading has changed, not the feeling you have about it. That happens, and when it does, you act once and plainly.

The rest is stirring. It feels like attention and it looks like work, which is why it is so hard to stop. A parent who checks on a sleeping child every five minutes wakes the child. The trader who checks on a trade every five minutes usually ends up waking it too.

## Try This

For the next ten trades, write the size, the stop and the target before you enter, and allow yourself to change them only once, and only if the reason for the trade is gone. Count every order you send on each idea. At the end, set the count beside what each idea paid, and look at what the two columns say.
