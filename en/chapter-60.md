# 60. Cooking a Small Fish

*Part II: The Virtue · 《治大国，若烹小鲜》*

## The Verse

治大国，若烹小鲜。

以道莅天下，其鬼不神；非其鬼不神，其神不伤人；非其神不伤人，圣人亦不伤人。

夫两不相伤，故德交归焉。

Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish.

When the Tao presides over the world, its ghosts lose their power. It is not that the ghosts lose their power; it is that their power does not harm people. It is not only that their power does not harm people; the sage does not harm them either.

When neither harms the other, virtue flows back to both.

*The Chinese is the received Wang Bi text. The translation is the author's own.*

## The Trader

1. Cook a small fish gently. Turn it too often and it falls apart in the pan. Manage a position the same way.

2. A swing position does not need you every hour. Look at it at the times you set, twice a day at most, and leave it alone in between.

3. Every look invites a touch. Every touch is a decision made with less thought than the one that opened the trade.

4. The stop was set when you were calm. Moving it closer because the screen is jumpy is turning the fish.

5. Moving it further away because you hope is also turning the fish, and worse.

6. When the Tao presides, the ghosts lose their power. The ghosts are the intraday wiggles, the rumours, the red number at eleven in the morning. They are always there.

7. It is not that the ghosts lose their power. It is that they no longer reach you, because nothing you do depends on them.

8. The sage does not harm the people either. The worst damage to a good position is usually done by its owner.

9. Set the alert at the stop and the target, and close the chart. The fish cooks without you.

10. When neither harms the other, virtue flows back. Leave the market to its noise, and the market leaves the plan alone.

## The Story

Anyone who grew up in a Chinese kitchen knows how a small fish is fried. You lay it in the hot oil and you leave it. You do not prod it to see how it is doing. When the underside is done, you turn it, once, and you leave it again. The cook who keeps lifting the edge to look ends up with a pan of broken fish and a good view of every stage of the breaking.

I had known that since childhood. It took me much longer to notice that I did not cook my positions that way.

---

The morning I noticed, I was holding a swing trade. The plan was the ordinary kind: entered by the rule, a stop and a target set before the order went out, and a holding period measured in days. Nothing about it required me during the session. The executor held the stop. The target would fill or it would not.

I opened the chart anyway, shortly after the open, to see how it was doing. Then again a little later. Somewhere in the middle of the morning I started to feel faintly ridiculous, and on an impulse I made a small mark in the notebook beside the keyboard. After that, every time I opened the chart, I added a mark. Beside each one I wrote a word if I had done anything while I was looking.

By the close the marks had run across one line and onto the next. That was not what bothered me. What bothered me was the words.

*Alert.* I had moved a price alert. *Note.* I had written myself a reminder about the position. *Stop.* Around eleven, the position had dipped, the number on the screen had turned red, and I had moved the stop closer. *Almost.* That one I had written after nearly selling outright, a little after the stop.

Not one of those touches had been planned. Each was a decision about the position, and each was made with less thought than the decision that had opened it. The trade had been designed on a quiet evening. It was being managed in passing, by someone glancing at a number.

---

The ending was the one you would expect. A day later, in the ordinary noise of an ordinary afternoon, the price came down to the closer stop and took the position out. It was not a large loss. It was a loss the rule had not agreed to, at a stop the rule had not placed. I had turned the fish until it came apart.

*Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish.* The emperor in that line has a great state and many ministers and a thousand things to do. The advice is still not to do more of them. It is to stop poking.

---

The rule that came out of it is the plainest on my card. A swing position is looked at twice a day, at times written down: once after the open has settled, once before the close. Not more. Between those times the chart is closed. The stop and the target sit with the executor, which does not get bored or worried and does not need to look.

The first week was harder than I expected. I would finish something, my hand would move toward the chart on its own, and I would have to take it back. I kept making the marks, now as a record of the looks I had not taken. The marks were for the urge. The urge, it turned out, came at the same times every day: just after the open, around the middle of the morning, and whenever the screen in the corner of my eye changed colour.

*When the Tao presides over the world, its ghosts lose their power.* The ghosts in a trading day are exactly those things. The red number at eleven. The rumour in the afternoon. The wiggle that means nothing. They did not go away when I closed the chart. They were still there every day. They simply no longer reached anything, because nothing I did depended on them.

---

What made the rule easy to keep, in the end, was the forward book.

The frozen rules on the desk are filled once a day, at the day's prices. Nobody watches them during the session. Nobody nudges their stops at eleven. For a season they are left completely alone, and at the end of the season their record means exactly what it says. One evening it occurred to me that the forward book was being managed better than my own swing trade had been, and that the only difference between them was that one had a cook and the other did not.

*It is not only that their power does not harm people; the sage does not harm them either.* The worst damage to that position had not been done by the market. The market had done what markets do. The damage had been done by its owner, with good intentions, at eleven in the morning.

---

I still look twice a day. On most days the second look is the more boring of the two, which is how I know the rule is working. Sometimes, when I am tempted to break it, I think of the pan, and of the cook who lifts the edge to see, and of how much that cook learns about frying fish, and how little fish is left at the end.
